Three DNS records decide whether your business emails reach the inbox or the spam folder. Here is what SPF, DKIM and DMARC do and how to set them up. (149 characters: shown in Google results)

If customers tell you your emails went to their spam folder, or an invoice you sent never arrived, the cause is very often three missing records in your domain's settings: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

They sound technical, but each one has a simple job. Together they prove to other email systems that a message really came from your business, and they make it much harder for anyone to send fake emails pretending to be you.

Why this matters now

Gmail, Yahoo and Outlook have all tightened their rules on incoming email over the last couple of years. Mail from a domain without these records is far more likely to be filtered as spam or rejected outright, even if you only send a handful of emails a day. If you use your own domain for email, it is worth checking all three are in place.

SPF: who is allowed to send as you

SPF (Sender Policy Framework) is a list of the services allowed to send email using your domain. When a message arrives, the receiving server checks whether it came from one of them.

  • You can only have one SPF record per domain, so every service has to go in the same record.
  • Include everything that sends as you: Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, your website's contact form, your invoicing software and any newsletter tool.
  • A missing service is one of the most common reasons legitimate emails fail.

DKIM: a signature on every email

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) adds a hidden digital signature to each email you send. The receiving server checks it against a public key published in your domain's settings. If the signature matches, it knows the message came from you and was not changed on the way.

Your email provider creates the keys; you add the records it gives you to your domain. In Microsoft 365 it is a couple of records and a switch to turn signing on.

DMARC: what to do when a check fails

DMARC ties the other two together. It tells receiving servers what to do with a message that fails SPF and DKIM, and it sends you reports showing who is sending email using your domain. That is often the first time a business discovers a forgotten service, or someone trying to impersonate them.

A DMARC policy has three settings:

  • none: just watch and report. This is where everyone should start.
  • quarantine: send failing messages to spam.
  • reject: refuse failing messages completely.

Where to start

Set up SPF and DKIM first, then add DMARC in monitoring mode. Read the reports for a few weeks, fix anything legitimate that is failing, and only then move to quarantine and, eventually, reject. Jumping straight to reject is the quickest way to block your own emails.

What phishing actually is

Phishing is an email, text or message pretending to come from someone you trust, such as your bank, a supplier, Microsoft, HMRC or even your own boss.   • Urgency or fear. "Your account will be closed today", "Final notice", "Payment overdue". Pressure is designed to stop you thinking.
  • A sender address that is almost right. Look past the display name at the actual address. microsoft-support@outlook-security.com is not Microsoft.

  • A request to log in. Genuine services rarely email you a link asking you to sign in to fix a problem. Go to the website yourself instead.
  • Unexpected attachments. Especially invoices, delivery notes, voicemails or "shared documents" you were not expecting.
  • Changed bank details. A supplier or contractor suddenly asking you to pay a new account is one of the most expensive scams around. Always check by phone, using a number you already have.

  • Keep the email, as it helps work out what happened

Training helps, but the strongest protection is making a stolen password useless on its own.

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for email and every important account. Even if a password is stolen, the attacker cannot sign in without the second step.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on your domain so criminals find it much harder to send emails pretending to be you. Our guide to business email explains why these matter.
  • Use an email service with proper filtering that catches most phishing before it reaches anyone's inbox.
  • Agree a simple rule that bank detail changes and urgent payment requests are always confirmed by phone.

Here are the most common reasons a small business website is slow, and what fixes them.

First, measure it

Before changing anything, find out how slow the site really is. Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool tests any page and shows its Core Web Vitals, the scores Google uses to judge how fast a page loads, how quickly it responds to taps, and whether things jump around while it loads. Test your home page and one or two other important pages, and pay most attention to the mobile results.

1. Huge images

This is the most common cause by far. A photo straight from a phone or camera can be several megabytes, and the page has to download all of it even if it is shown the size of a postage stamp. Images should be resized to the size they are displayed at, compressed, and saved in a modern format such as WebP. Images further down the page should only load as the visitor scrolls to them.

2. Too many plugins

Each plugin can add its own scripts, styles and database work to every page. A site with forty plugins, several of which do the same job, will rarely be fast. Review what you have, remove anything you do not need, and replace heavy plugins with lighter ones where you can.

3. Cheap or overcrowded hosting

On budget shared hosting, your site shares a server with hundreds of others. When one of them gets busy, everyone slows down. If your site is slow even on a simple page, the server may simply be too slow or too crowded to do the job.

4. No caching

Without caching, WordPress builds every page from scratch for every visitor, running the same code and database queries again and again. Page caching saves a ready-made copy and serves that instead, which can make a dramatic difference.

5. An old version of PHP

PHP is the language WordPress runs on. Each newer version is noticeably faster and more secure than the last. Many sites are still running versions that are years out of date, simply because nobody has changed the setting. Check your plugins support the newer version, take a backup, then switch.

6. Heavy themes and page builders

Some themes and page builders load a huge amount of code to offer every possible feature, even on pages that use none of them. A theme built for your site, using only what it needs, is often several times lighter.

7. Too many outside scripts

Chat widgets, social media feeds, tracking tags, embedded maps and videos all load from other companies' servers, and each one adds delay. Keep the ones that earn their place and remove the rest.

Quick wins you can try today

  • Resize and compress the largest images on your home page
  • Delete plugins that are switched off or no longer needed
  • Check which PHP version your hosting is using
  • Run PageSpeed Insights again after each change to see what helped

If you have bid for a public sector contract, filled in a supplier questionnaire or renewed a cyber insurance policy recently, you may have been asked whether you have Cyber Essentials. It is a UK government-backed scheme that shows your business has the basic protections in place against the most common online attacks. Here is what it involves, in plain English.

What Cyber Essentials is

Cyber Essentials was created by the government, and is run by the National Cyber Security Centre with its delivery partner IASME. It sets out five basic technical controls that, together, stop the large majority of everyday attacks, such as phishing, password guessing and attacks on software that has not been updated. Businesses that meet them can be certified, and certificates are renewed every year.

The two levels

  • Cyber Essentials is a self-assessment. You answer a set of questions about how your IT is set up, and a board director or equivalent signs it off. An assessor from a certification body then reviews your answers.
  • Cyber Essentials Plus covers the same controls, but an assessor also tests your systems hands on, checking a sample of your devices and accounts to confirm they really are set up the way you said.

The five controls

These are the five areas the assessment covers. None of them needs expensive equipment. Most come down to setting things up properly and keeping them that way.

1. Firewalls

Every device that connects to the internet should be protected by a correctly configured firewall, both at the edge of your office network and on laptops used elsewhere. Default router passwords must be changed.

2. Secure configuration

Computers, phones and software should be set up securely rather than left on their factory settings. That means removing software and accounts you do not need, changing default passwords, and having devices lock when they are not in use.

3. Security updates

Operating systems and software must be supported by their makers and kept updated. Critical and high-risk security updates need to be installed within 14 days of being released, which in practice means turning on automatic updates wherever you can. Old software that no longer receives updates, such as an out-of-date version of Windows, has to be replaced or removed.

4. User access control

Everyone should have their own account, with only the access they need for their job. Administrator accounts should only be used for administrative tasks, not everyday email and browsing. Multi-factor authentication must be turned on for cloud services such as Microsoft 365 wherever it is available.

5. Malware protection

Devices need protection against viruses and other malicious software. On most modern computers this is built in, such as Microsoft Defender on Windows, as long as it is switched on and kept updated.

Why it is worth doing

  • Some government and public sector contracts require it
  • Larger customers increasingly ask their suppliers for it
  • It can help with cyber insurance, and some small businesses that certify qualify for insurance included with the certificate
  • Most importantly, the controls genuinely protect you against the attacks small businesses face most often

How to get started

Certification is carried out by an accredited certification body, and the questions are published so you can see exactly what will be asked before you apply. Most of the work happens beforehand, making sure your devices, accounts and cloud services actually meet the controls. For a small business with a handful of computers, that is very achievable.

Choosing where your servers live is a decision you will live with for years. Moving hardware is disruptive, so it pays to ask the right questions before you sign anything. Here are the ten we would ask if we were looking for colocation in Newcastle upon Tyne, and what good answers sound like.

1. Where is it, and can you get there?

Location matters more than people expect. When a disk fails or you need to swap a server, you want to reach the building quickly. Check how far it is from your office, whether there is parking, and how close it is to public transport. A data centre a short drive away is far more useful than a cheaper one at the other end of the country.

2. Can you visit whenever you need to?

Problems do not keep office hours. Ask whether you get 24/7 access, how you get in, and whether you need to book visits in advance. Proximity fob access to the data hall is a good sign that access is controlled but not awkward.

3. What happens when the power fails?

Power is the heart of any data centre. Good answers include an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) with spare capacity, usually described as N+1, a standby generator that starts automatically, and enough fuel stored on site to run for days, not hours. Ask how often the generator is tested on load. If your equipment has two power supplies, ask for A and B feeds so one failure does not take your server down.

4. How is it cooled?

Servers produce a lot of heat, and overheating shortens their life. Look for precision cooling with spare capacity (again, N+1), contained hot or cold aisles so cool air goes where it is needed, and temperature monitoring across the room.

5. How is it protected from fire?

Water and servers do not mix. Many data centres use inert gas suppression systems, which put out a fire without damaging equipment. Ask what system is used and how the room is monitored for early signs of smoke.

6. How secure is it?

Your hardware holds your data, so physical security matters. Look for a perimeter fence, monitored CCTV, controlled entry to the data hall, and a clear record of who has been in and when.

7. What connectivity is available?

Ask how many separate fibre routes come into the building, so a single cut cable does not take the whole site offline. Check how much bandwidth is included, what happens if you need more, and whether DDoS protection and reverse DNS are part of the service.

8. Is there someone on hand to help?

Remote hands means staff at the data centre can do simple physical tasks for you, such as pressing a power button, checking lights or swapping a cable, so you do not have to travel in for every small job. Ask whether it is included in the price or charged by the hour.

9. Can you grow without moving?

You may start with a single server, but needs change. Check whether the provider can offer more rack units, a quarter rack or a full rack in the same building later, so growing does not mean moving.

10. Is the pricing clear?

Ask for the full monthly price, including power, bandwidth and IP addresses, and whether there is a setup fee. Be wary of low headline prices with extra charges for things you will definitely need.

Every website needs somewhere to live. That place is called hosting, and while it sounds technical, the idea is simple. This guide explains what hosting is, what the terms on a hosting plan actually mean, and how to choose the right one.

What hosting actually is

A website is a collection of files and, usually, a database. To be seen by anyone, those files need to sit on a computer that is always switched on and connected to the internet. That computer is a server, and hosting is renting space on one. When someone types your web address, their browser asks your host's server for the pages and shows them.

Domain and hosting are different things

Your domain is your address, such as yourbusiness.co.uk. Your hosting is the building at that address. You need both, and they can come from the same company or different ones. Your domain's DNS settings tell the internet where your hosting is.

What the terms on a hosting plan mean

  • Hosted websites. How many separate websites you can run on the plan. Handy if you have more than one business or project.
  • Storage. The space for your website's files, images and database. SSD storage is faster than older hard drives, which helps your pages load quickly. Most small business sites use far less than people expect.
  • Databases. WordPress and most modern websites keep their content in a MySQL database. Each website usually needs one.
  • Bandwidth. How much data your site can send to visitors each month. Every page view uses a little. A typical small business site needs only a few gigabytes.
  • Email addresses. Mailboxes on your own domain, such as hello@yourbusiness.co.uk, which look far more professional than a free email address.
  • SSL certificate. What puts the padlock next to your address and keeps information sent through your site secure. It should be included.

Types of hosting

  • Shared hosting. Your site shares a server with others. Affordable and perfectly good for most small business websites, as long as the server is not overcrowded.
  • Managed hosting. The host looks after the technical side for you, such as updates and backups.
  • VPS and dedicated servers. A slice of a server, or a whole one, for busy sites and specialist software.
  • Colocation. Your own server hardware, kept in a professional data centre.

How to choose

  • Where are the servers? UK hosting is faster for UK visitors.
  • Can you speak to a real person when something goes wrong?
  • Is email included, and how many addresses?
  • Are backups taken, and can you restore them?
  • Is the price clear, including what it costs after any introductory offer?

A step-by-step guide to moving your website and email to a new host without breaking anything, from backups and testing to DNS and switching over. (146 characters: shown in Google results)

Moving your website to a new host sounds risky. Done carelessly, it can mean a broken site, missing emails or a few days offline. Done properly, your visitors will never notice. Here is the step-by-step process we follow, in plain English.

Why people move

Common reasons include a slow website, prices that went up after an introductory offer, poor support, or wanting everything in one place with a local team. Whatever the reason, the process is the same.

Before you start

  • Find your logins. You will need access to your current hosting, your website's admin area, and wherever your domain is registered.
  • Check what is hosted where. Is your email with the same company as your website? If so, it needs moving too, and it is the part most likely to go wrong.
  • Do not cancel anything yet. Keep your old hosting running until the move is finished and checked.

Step 1: Take a full backup

Download a complete copy of your website's files and its database. For WordPress, that means the whole site folder and a database export. Keep it somewhere safe. If anything goes wrong, this is your way back.

Step 2: Copy the site to the new host

Upload the files to the new hosting, create a new database, and import your database into it. Then update the website's settings so it connects to the new database.

Step 3: Test before anyone sees it

This is the step people skip. Before changing anything public, check the site on the new host using a temporary address or a setting on your own computer that points your domain at the new server just for you. Click through every page, test your forms, and check images load.

Step 4: Move your email carefully

If your email is moving too, create the same mailboxes on the new service first, then copy across existing messages. Email depends on DNS records called MX records, plus the SPF, DKIM and DMARC records that stop your messages going to spam. Get these right before you switch.

Step 5: Lower the DNS refresh time

DNS records have a setting called TTL that tells the internet how long to remember them. A day or two before the move, lower it to a few minutes. Then, when you switch, the change spreads quickly instead of taking hours.

Step 6: Switch over

Update your DNS so your domain points at the new host. Within a short time, visitors start arriving at the new server. Make sure the SSL certificate is working on the new host so the padlock shows straight away.

Step 7: Check, then wait before cancelling

Check the site, forms and email again from a few different devices. Keep the old hosting for at least a week in case anything was missed. Only cancel it once you are sure everything is working.
