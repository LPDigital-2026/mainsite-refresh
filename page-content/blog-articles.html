Three DNS records decide whether your business emails reach the inbox or the spam folder. Here is what SPF, DKIM and DMARC do and how to set them up. (149 characters: shown in Google results)

If customers tell you your emails went to their spam folder, or an invoice you sent never arrived, the cause is very often three missing records in your domain's settings: SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

They sound technical, but each one has a simple job. Together they prove to other email systems that a message really came from your business, and they make it much harder for anyone to send fake emails pretending to be you.

Why this matters now

Gmail, Yahoo and Outlook have all tightened their rules on incoming email over the last couple of years. Mail from a domain without these records is far more likely to be filtered as spam or rejected outright, even if you only send a handful of emails a day. If you use your own domain for email, it is worth checking all three are in place.

SPF: who is allowed to send as you

SPF (Sender Policy Framework) is a list of the services allowed to send email using your domain. When a message arrives, the receiving server checks whether it came from one of them.

  • You can only have one SPF record per domain, so every service has to go in the same record.
  • Include everything that sends as you: Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, your website's contact form, your invoicing software and any newsletter tool.
  • A missing service is one of the most common reasons legitimate emails fail.

DKIM: a signature on every email

DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) adds a hidden digital signature to each email you send. The receiving server checks it against a public key published in your domain's settings. If the signature matches, it knows the message came from you and was not changed on the way.

Your email provider creates the keys; you add the records it gives you to your domain. In Microsoft 365 it is a couple of records and a switch to turn signing on.

DMARC: what to do when a check fails

DMARC ties the other two together. It tells receiving servers what to do with a message that fails SPF and DKIM, and it sends you reports showing who is sending email using your domain. That is often the first time a business discovers a forgotten service, or someone trying to impersonate them.

A DMARC policy has three settings:

  • none: just watch and report. This is where everyone should start.
  • quarantine: send failing messages to spam.
  • reject: refuse failing messages completely.

Where to start

Set up SPF and DKIM first, then add DMARC in monitoring mode. Read the reports for a few weeks, fix anything legitimate that is failing, and only then move to quarantine and, eventually, reject. Jumping straight to reject is the quickest way to block your own emails.

Microsoft 365 comes in three main business plans: Business Basic, Business Standard and Business Premium. They share the same core, so the question is not which one is best, but which one your business actually needs. Here is the difference in plain English.

What every plan includes

  • Professional email on your own domain, with a 50 GB mailbox per user
  • Teams for chat, calls and video meetings
  • 1 TB of OneDrive storage per user, plus SharePoint for shared files
  • Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook in a web browser and on phones and tablets

Business Basic: email, Teams and storage

Basic suits teams that mostly work in email and a browser. You get everything above, but not the installed desktop versions of Word, Excel and PowerPoint. If your staff already have Office on their computers, or rarely do more than read and edit documents, Basic is often all you need.

Business Standard: the full desktop apps

Standard adds the installed desktop apps, which each user can install on up to five PCs or Macs, five tablets and five phones. It also adds webinar tools and Bookings, which lets customers book appointments with you online. This is the plan most small offices choose, because the desktop apps are what people expect to use every day.

Business Premium: Standard plus security

Premium includes everything in Standard, plus Microsoft's business security tools:

  • Defender for Business, which protects laptops and computers against malware and ransomware
  • Intune, which manages your devices: you can enforce passwords and encryption, and wipe a lost laptop remotely
  • Conditional access, which controls who can sign in, from where and on which devices

Premium makes sense if you handle sensitive client information, have staff working from home on company laptops, or need to show insurers or customers that your devices are managed and protected.

How to choose

  • Email and Teams, mostly in a browser: Business Basic
  • You want the full Office apps on your computers: Business Standard
  • You also need devices managed and properly protected: Business Premium

You can also mix plans, giving Standard to office staff and Basic to people who only need email. Plans can be changed later as your needs grow.

Colocation moves your own servers into a professional data centre. How it works, how it compares with the cloud and the office, and who it suits. (145 characters: shown in Google results)

If your business runs its own servers, they probably live in a cupboard, a back office or under someone's desk. Colocation means moving that equipment into a professional data centre, where it gets proper power, cooling, security and connectivity, while you keep full ownership and control of it.

How colocation works

You rent space in a rack, from a single unit for one server up to a full rack, and bring your own hardware. The data centre provides the building, the power, the cooling and the network connection. Your servers, your software and your data stay exactly as they are; they just live somewhere far better suited to them.

Colocation, cloud or the office?

  • In the office: cheap to start, but exposed to power cuts, overheating, break-ins and slow office broadband.
  • In the cloud: no hardware to own, but monthly costs can climb quickly for steady, always-on workloads, and you are tied to one provider.
  • Colocation: you keep your own hardware and costs stay predictable, with data-centre reliability.

What you get in a data centre

  • Backup power: UPS systems and a standby generator that take over if the mains fails
  • Precision cooling, so your equipment runs at the right temperature all year
  • Fire suppression designed for IT equipment, using gas rather than water
  • Physical security: fencing, CCTV, alarms and controlled access
  • Fast, resilient connectivity over diverse fibre routes

Who it suits

Colocation is a good fit if you run your own servers for websites, business applications, backups, private cloud or game servers, and you want them to be more reliable without handing everything over to a cloud provider. It is also a sensible home for a second copy of your data, away from the office, in case of fire, flood or theft.

Waiting updates, missing backups and slow pages all add up. Five warning signs your WordPress site needs attention, and what good maintenance looks like. (153 characters: shown in Google results)

A WordPress website is not something you build once and forget. Behind the scenes it is made of WordPress itself, a theme and a collection of plugins, and all of them are updated regularly. Leave them alone for long enough and problems start to appear. Here are the signs your site needs some attention.

1. You see "updates available" every time you log in

Many updates fix security holes. Out-of-date plugins are one of the most common ways WordPress sites get hacked, so a long list of waiting updates is a risk, not just an inconvenience.

2. You are not sure when it was last backed up

A backup is only useful if it exists, is recent and can actually be restored. If you do not know where your backups are kept or when the last one ran, assume you do not have one.

3. It has slowed down

Sites tend to get slower over time as plugins pile up and old ones stop being looked after. Slow pages frustrate visitors and can count against you in search results.

4. Something has quietly stopped working

A contact form that no longer sends, a gallery that will not load, a layout broken on phones. These problems are easy to miss if nobody checks, and every missed enquiry is a lost customer.

5. Your browser shows a security warning

If your SSL certificate lapses, browsers warn visitors that your site is not secure, and most will leave immediately. Certificates now need renewing more often than they used to, so this is worth monitoring.

What good maintenance looks like

  • Regular updates to WordPress, plugins and the theme, checked afterwards so nothing breaks
  • Automatic backups, stored away from the website, and checked
  • Security checks and monitoring
  • Someone to call when something goes wrong

A PBX (private branch exchange) is the system that runs a business phone setup: extensions, transferring calls, hunt groups, voicemail, call queues, opening hours and on-hold music. A missed domain or SSL renewal can take your website and email offline overnight. How to make sure it never happens to your business. (133 characters: shown in Google results)

Two small renewals keep your business online: your domain name and your SSL certificate. Miss either one and the effect is immediate. Your website disappears or shows a security warning, and with a lapsed domain your email can stop too. Here is how to make sure it never happens to you.

What happens when a domain expires

Your domain is rented, usually a year at a time. If the renewal fails, often because a card on file has expired or a reminder went to an old email address, your website and email stop working. There is normally a short grace period to renew, but after that the domain can be released, and some people buy up lapsed domains to resell them at a high price.

How to protect your domain

  • Turn on auto-renew, and keep the payment card up to date
  • Make sure renewal reminders go to an inbox someone actually reads, not a former employee's address
  • Register the domain in the business's name, and keep the login details somewhere safe
  • Consider renewing for several years at a time

What an SSL certificate does

An SSL certificate is what puts the padlock next to your web address and encrypts information moving between your site and its visitors, such as contact form details. When it expires, browsers show a full-page warning that your site is not secure, and most visitors leave immediately.

Certificates are getting shorter

The industry is steadily reducing how long certificates last, so they have to be renewed more often. Certificates used to last over a year; they are now limited to around six months, and that will keep falling over the next few years. Renewing by hand is no longer realistic. Certificates should renew automatically, and something should check that they actually did.
