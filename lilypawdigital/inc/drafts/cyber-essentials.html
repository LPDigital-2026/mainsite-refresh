If you have bid for a public sector contract, filled in a supplier questionnaire or renewed a cyber insurance policy recently, you may have been asked whether you have Cyber Essentials. It is a UK government-backed scheme that shows your business has the basic protections in place against the most common online attacks. Here is what it involves, in plain English.

What Cyber Essentials is

Cyber Essentials was created by the government, and is run by the National Cyber Security Centre with its delivery partner IASME. It sets out five basic technical controls that, together, stop the large majority of everyday attacks, such as phishing, password guessing and attacks on software that has not been updated. Businesses that meet them can be certified, and certificates are renewed every year.

The two levels

  • Cyber Essentials is a self-assessment. You answer a set of questions about how your IT is set up, and a board director or equivalent signs it off. An assessor from a certification body then reviews your answers.
  • Cyber Essentials Plus covers the same controls, but an assessor also tests your systems hands on, checking a sample of your devices and accounts to confirm they really are set up the way you said.

The five controls

These are the five areas the assessment covers. None of them needs expensive equipment. Most come down to setting things up properly and keeping them that way.

1. Firewalls

Every device that connects to the internet should be protected by a correctly configured firewall, both at the edge of your office network and on laptops used elsewhere. Default router passwords must be changed.

2. Secure configuration

Computers, phones and software should be set up securely rather than left on their factory settings. That means removing software and accounts you do not need, changing default passwords, and having devices lock when they are not in use.

3. Security updates

Operating systems and software must be supported by their makers and kept updated. Critical and high-risk security updates need to be installed within 14 days of being released, which in practice means turning on automatic updates wherever you can. Old software that no longer receives updates, such as an out-of-date version of Windows, has to be replaced or removed.

4. User access control

Everyone should have their own account, with only the access they need for their job. Administrator accounts should only be used for administrative tasks, not everyday email and browsing. Multi-factor authentication must be turned on for cloud services such as Microsoft 365 wherever it is available.

5. Malware protection

Devices need protection against viruses and other malicious software. On most modern computers this is built in, such as Microsoft Defender on Windows, as long as it is switched on and kept updated.

Why it is worth doing

  • Some government and public sector contracts require it
  • Larger customers increasingly ask their suppliers for it
  • It can help with cyber insurance, and some small businesses that certify qualify for insurance included with the certificate
  • Most importantly, the controls genuinely protect you against the attacks small businesses face most often

How to get started

Certification is carried out by an accredited certification body, and the questions are published so you can see exactly what will be asked before you apply. Most of the work happens beforehand, making sure your devices, accounts and cloud services actually meet the controls. For a small business with a handful of computers, that is very achievable.
