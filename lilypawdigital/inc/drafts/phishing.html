Most cyber attacks on small businesses do not start with clever hacking. They start with an email. The government's annual Cyber Security Breaches Survey finds phishing is by far the most common attack UK businesses face, and it only takes one person clicking the wrong link on a busy afternoon. The good news is that phishing emails share the same giveaways, and a team that knows them is much harder to catch out.

What phishing actually is

Phishing is an email, text or message pretending to come from someone you trust, such as your bank, a supplier, Microsoft, HMRC or even your own boss. The aim is to get you to do one thing: type a password into a fake login page, open an attachment that installs something nasty, or send money to the wrong account.

Eight warning signs

  • Urgency or fear. "Your account will be closed today", "Final notice", "Payment overdue". Pressure is designed to stop you thinking.
  • A sender address that is almost right. Look past the display name at the actual address. microsoft-support@outlook-security.com is not Microsoft.
  • Links that go somewhere unexpected. On a computer, hover over a link before clicking and check where it really leads. On a phone, press and hold.
  • A request to log in. Genuine services rarely email you a link asking you to sign in to fix a problem. Go to the website yourself instead.
  • Unexpected attachments. Especially invoices, delivery notes, voicemails or "shared documents" you were not expecting.
  • Changed bank details. A supplier or contractor suddenly asking you to pay a new account is one of the most expensive scams around. Always check by phone, using a number you already have.
  • Generic greetings. "Dear customer" from a company that knows your name is a warning sign, although targeted scams will often use your name.
  • Something that just feels off. An odd tone, a strange time of day, a colleague who never emails like that. Trust that instinct.

What to do if you are not sure

Do not click, reply or open anything. Contact the person or company another way, using details you already have, and ask. In the UK you can forward suspicious emails to the National Cyber Security Centre at report@phishing.gov.uk, and suspicious texts to 7726. Then delete the message.

What to do if someone has already clicked

It happens to careful people too, and the worst response is to keep quiet. Speed matters far more than blame.

  • Change the password for the account straight away, and anywhere else the same password was used
  • Tell whoever looks after your IT so they can check for unusual sign-ins and forwarding rules
  • If bank details were shared or a payment was made, call your bank immediately
  • Keep the email, as it helps work out what happened

Make phishing less likely to work

Training helps, but the strongest protection is making a stolen password useless on its own.

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for email and every important account. Even if a password is stolen, the attacker cannot sign in without the second step.
  • Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC on your domain so criminals find it much harder to send emails pretending to be you. Our guide to business email explains why these matter.
  • Use an email service with proper filtering that catches most phishing before it reaches anyone's inbox.
  • Agree a simple rule that bank detail changes and urgent payment requests are always confirmed by phone.
